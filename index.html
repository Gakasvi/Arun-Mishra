Home / Insights / Input credit

The seven places input credit quietly disappears

Indirect tax · 12 min read

When a large corporate loses input credit, the loss almost never comes from one bad decision. It accumulates through small gaps in ownership — points where a task belongs to procurement in practice, to accounts payable on the org chart, and to the tax team in consequence.

The leakage is not hidden. It is unowned.

1. Vendor filings that were never reconciled at invoice level

Summary-level reconciliation will tell you the totals roughly agree. It will not tell you which specific invoices your vendor failed to report, which is the only level at which you can act — by withholding payment, by following up, or by writing the credit off deliberately rather than by default.

2. Credit blocked by documentation, not by law

Eligible credit gets reversed because the supporting document was incomplete at the time of review. The transaction was fine; the evidence was assembled months later by someone who wasn't there.

3. Job work and movement of goods

In manufacturing, credit chains break at plant boundaries. Goods move, documentation follows late, and the timing mismatch is resolved by reversing rather than investigating.

4. Credit notes issued commercially, not fiscally

A commercial team agrees a rebate. A credit note is issued. The tax treatment is applied to a different period, or not at all, and the position on both sides stops agreeing.

5. Refunds nobody owns

Export refunds and inverted duty accumulations sit as balances rather than as receivables. No role has the filing on their calendar, so the balance grows until someone treats it as a project.

6. Rate and classification applied inconsistently across entities

Group entities take different positions on the same transaction type, usually because each was decided locally at different times. Whichever position is wrong, one of them creates exposure and the other creates lost credit.

7. Reconciliation performed but never reviewed

The most expensive failure of all. A reconciliation is produced monthly, filed, and never read. Breaks age for years inside a control that technically exists.

What to do about it

  • Reconcile at invoice level, monthly, with a named owner and an escalation rule
  • Capture documentation at transaction time, tied to the transaction record
  • Put refund filings on the compliance calendar with an owner, like any return
  • Standardise classification positions across entities and document the reasoning
  • Review reconciliations. A control nobody reads is not a control

Why your close takes eighteen days

Controllership · 9 min read

Ask a controller why the close takes as long as it does, and the answer is usually volume. Measure it, and the answer is almost always waiting.

Map the dependencies before you touch anything

Most close calendars are lists of tasks with dates. They are not dependency maps, so nobody can see which single late input is holding six downstream tasks. Until you can see that, every improvement effort is guesswork.

The three structural causes

  • Sequential work that could be parallel. Entities closing one after another because one team does all of them.
  • Reconciliations performed after the fact. Anything reconciled only at month-end turns the close into an investigation.
  • Review as a bottleneck. One person approving everything, in order, at the busiest time of the month.

What changes the number

Continuous reconciliation moves work out of the close window. Standard journal templates with validation remove the correction loop. Exception-based review means the reviewer looks at breaks rather than at everything. Automating the reporting pack removes two days that were never accounting work in the first place.

A six-day close in a multi-entity group is normal, not aspirational. Eighteen days means the work is being done at the wrong time, not that there is too much of it.

What to automate before you replace the ERP

Automation · 8 min read

ERP replacement is the most expensive answer to questions that usually have cheaper ones. It is sometimes the right answer. It is rarely the first one.

Start with the work that happens outside the system

Look at where your team actually spends time: invoice capture, matching, reconciliation, follow-up email, and building the reporting pack. Almost none of that requires a new ERP. It requires workflow, validation and integration around the ERP you already own.

The order that works

  • Standardise the chart of accounts and the accounting manual first
  • Automate reconciliations, because they produce the data quality everything else depends on
  • Then payables: capture, matching and exception routing
  • Then receivables: billing, dunning and cash application
  • Then reporting, which is mostly assembly once the ledger is trustworthy

When replacement is genuinely the answer

When the system cannot enforce segregation of duties, cannot support your entity structure, or has been customised past the point where it can be upgraded safely. Those are architectural limits. Everything else is a process problem wearing a systems costume.

What diligence finds in the finance function

Transactions · 10 min read

Financial diligence is more predictable than most sellers expect. Buyers look at the same short list, and the findings that reduce valuation are usually the ones that could have been fixed in a quarter.

The nine things they test

  • Quality of earnings: what is recurring, and what was helped by timing
  • Revenue recognition policy and its consistency across periods
  • Working capital: the true normalised level, not the year-end snapshot
  • Provisions and their adequacy, especially litigation and receivables
  • Tax exposure: open assessments, positions taken, documentation behind them
  • Related-party transactions and their pricing basis
  • Off-balance-sheet commitments and contingent liabilities
  • Controls: segregation of duties, access, override history
  • Reconciliation status and the age of unresolved breaks

What you can fix before the process starts

Clear aged reconciliation breaks. Document your tax positions with the evidence that supports them. Rebuild the related-party file. Set provisions on a stated policy rather than judgement. None of this is difficult. It is simply work that never had a deadline until now.

The three you usually cannot fix late — quality of earnings, revenue policy history and genuine working capital levels — are the reason to start a year out rather than a month out.

Controls that hold when volume triples

Controls · 7 min read

A control environment designed for today's volume is a control environment with an expiry date. The useful test is simple: if transaction volume tripled next quarter, which controls would survive?

Manual controls fail silently

A reviewer checking every invoice works at a thousand a month. At three thousand, the checking becomes cursory, and nothing in the process announces that it has stopped being a control. The documentation still says it happens. It still happens. It just no longer catches anything.

Three properties of a control that scales

  • System-enforced. The transaction cannot proceed if the condition is not met.
  • Exception-based. People review what broke, not what passed.
  • Evidenced automatically. The audit trail is a by-product, not a separate task.

Where to start

Take your risk and control matrix and mark every control that depends on a person looking at every item. That is your list, in order of transaction volume. Convert the top five, and you will have covered most of the risk that growth creates.

Confidentiality & data security

We work with your ledgers, contracts, tax filings and payroll. The handling standards below apply to every mandate and are written into every engagement letter.

NDA before data

A mutual non-disclosure agreement is signed before any client information is shared, including during the first scoping conversation.

Least-privilege access

Only named team members on the mandate receive access. Read-only wherever the work permits it. Access is revoked at engagement close, with a record of removal.

Client-controlled environments

Where you prefer it, we work entirely inside your systems and your data never leaves your environment. We have run full mandates this way.

Encryption in transit and at rest

Data exchange happens over encrypted channels through a secure repository. Ad-hoc email attachments of sensitive data are not used.

Device and endpoint controls

Firm-managed devices with full-disk encryption, screen lock, endpoint protection and no client data on removable media.

Defined retention

Working papers are retained only as long as professional and statutory requirements need. Client data is returned or destroyed on request, with written confirmation.

Independence and conflicts

  • We run a conflict check before accepting any mandate and will decline where a conflict exists
  • We accept no commission, referral fee or revenue share from software or service vendors
  • Every team member signs an individual confidentiality undertaking on joining
  • Sector insight is shared only in de-identified form, never with client-identifiable detail
  • We will not name you as a client, publicly or in a proposal, without your written consent

Questions we are asked before every mandate

If yours is not here, ask it directly. We would rather answer it now than during a proposal.

How is this different from what our statutory or internal auditors do?
Auditors test whether what happened was recorded correctly. We test whether the process itself is costing you money, and then we change it. Our output is a redesigned function, not an opinion.
Will this disrupt our team during month-end?
We schedule interviews and testing around your close calendar and take data extracts rather than your controller's time. Most of the effort is ours.
How much of our team's time does a mandate need?
Roughly two to four hours per process owner during the first fortnight, then a fortnightly decision forum of forty-five minutes. Automation phases need more involvement from the process owners of the workflows being built.
Do you replace our ERP?
Only if its architecture genuinely cannot support your structure or controls. We take no vendor fees, so we have no incentive to recommend replacement.
Can fees be linked to recovery?
On specific tax recovery mandates where a baseline can be measured objectively, yes. For transformation work we use fixed fees by phase, because outcome-linked fees distort scope.
Who will actually do the work?
The partner who scopes your mandate runs it, with a tax lead and an automation lead named in the engagement letter. You will meet them before you sign.
What happens at the end?
Documentation, training and a defined support taper. If a retainer stops producing measurable value, we will tell you to end it.
Will you sign our NDA, DPA and vendor agreements?
Yes. We work to client paper routinely and complete security questionnaires as standard.
Do you work outside India?
We work with Indian corporates including their overseas subsidiaries, coordinating with local advisors where local filing expertise is required.
What size of company do you work with?
Our work pays for itself above roughly ₹250Cr of turnover or a comparable transaction volume. Below that, we will usually tell you so.
